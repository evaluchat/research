# The contribution ladder — how teachers contribute to research

## The problem

Classroom teachers hold the evidence this field needs, but conventional research publishing asks them to write papers. The contribution ladder is the alternative: research participation as a series of escalating, well-defined steps, each valuable on its own, none requiring a paper.

## The ladder

| Rung | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | A noticed pattern in your own classroom — no formal method required | "My students who talk through their plan before drafting seem to revise more." |
| Documented experience | The same observation written down with enough context to be interpretable by others — a completed evidence bundle | An evidence bundle: context, intervention, observations (your narrative, verbatim), results, reflection, limitations, provenance |
| Structured experiment | A deliberate comparison with a defined measure, ideally pre-registered against a research question | "I set the drafting threshold at 5 contributions for one class and 10 for another, and compared independent writing after four weeks." |
| Replication / challenge | Trying the same thing in a different context, or reporting an opposite result | "I ran the same threshold comparison with beginner learners and got the opposite result." |
| Synthesis | Combining multiple contributions into a broader pattern | "Across three classrooms and two languages, the pattern that survives is that explanations, not message counts, predict revision quality." |
| Finding | A tiered claim approved through the review protocol | A provisional, tentative, or supported finding in the findings registry |

## The principle

The ladder is powered by a simple exchange:

> *I tried this → I tried it too → I got the opposite result → here is what appears to explain the difference.*

Every rung above observation is a variation on this exchange. Replications and challenges — including negative results — are as valuable as first observations, because they are what turn a single classroom's experience into a claim other people can rely on.

## How it maps to evidence bundles

Every evidence contribution is a mini-bundle (see the [evidence-bundle template](../templates/evidence-bundle/index.md)) and carries a `stage:` field naming its ladder rung. The evidence registry ([evidence/index.md](../evidence/index.md)) is organised by research question and contribution stage, so a reader can see at a glance which questions have observations and which have structured experiments.

## Relationship to findings tiers

The ladder describes how contributions are produced; the review protocol describes how they become claims. A finding's tier (provisional / tentative / supported) depends on the evidence behind it, not on the contributor's status — see [Claim governance — review protocol for findings](../governance/review-protocol.en.md).

## Negative results are valuable

A documented opposite result is not a failure; it is a boundary condition on someone else's finding. When the protocol's machinery is working, a challenge with evidence is the most informative contribution a teacher can make. Please report what did not work, and what you think explains it.
